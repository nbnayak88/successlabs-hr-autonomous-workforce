# Presales Strategy Lab — General Handbook

**Lab:** Presales Strategy Lab (PSL)  
**Track:** 11 — Presales  
**Learning Intent:** **INFLUENCE**  
**Maturity Stage:** Advanced  
**Core Intention:** Learn how to communicate value and influence business decisions.  
**Student Outcome:** Develop a consulting and client-facing mindset.

> **Influence is the bridge between brilliance and business.** PSL develops the ability to understand a client's problem, shape a credible solution narrative, communicate genuine value and help stakeholders make informed decisions.

---

# 1. Purpose

PSL converts technical and business capability into **client-facing value communication**.

The presales value loop is:

**Discover Client Need → Understand Context → Shape Solution → Position Value → Demonstrate → Handle Concerns → Build Confidence → Enable Decision**

Presales is not simply a product demonstration. It is the discipline of **connecting a client's problem to a credible future state**.

---

# 2. PSL Philosophy

1. **Influence is your intent** — communicate value and influence business decisions.
2. **Lead with the client's problem** — start with the business need, not the product.
3. **Tell stories, show outcomes** — demonstrate the future, not merely features.
4. **Position value, not price** — establish business value before commercial discussion.
5. **Listen to influence** — understanding precedes persuasion.
6. **Operate at the Advanced level** — bring consulting maturity and client empathy.
7. **Influence across the 11 GICS sectors** — decision drivers vary by industry.
8. **Influence serves the mission** — persuasion should remain grounded in truth and value.

# 5. Client Discovery Canvas

Before positioning a solution, establish:

| Dimension | Questions |
|---|---|
| Business Context | What is happening in the client's business? |
| Strategic Driver | Why does this matter now? |
| Problem | What problem must be solved? |
| Impact | What is the consequence of leaving it unresolved? |
| Stakeholders | Who experiences the problem? |
| Decision Makers | Who owns the decision? |
| Constraints | What limits the solution? |
| Current State | How does the client operate today? |
| Target State | What future experience is desired? |
| Capability Gap | What is missing? |
| Solution Fit | How could the proposed capability address it? |
| Value | What measurable or strategic outcomes could result? |
| Risk | What could prevent success? |
| Next Decision | What decision or action should follow? |

> **Discovery is not interrogation. It is structured listening.**

---

# 6. Problem-First Positioning

Use:

**Problem → Impact → Desired Outcome → Capability → Solution → Evidence → Value**

Avoid:

**Product → Features → Demo → Price**

The first sequence creates a business conversation.

The second risks creating a feature conversation.

---

# 7. Value Proposition Architecture

A value proposition should answer:

### For whom?
Who experiences the problem?

### What problem?
What needs to change?

### What capability?
What capability addresses it?

### How?
How does the solution enable the change?

### What outcome?
What improves?

### What evidence?
Why should the client believe the claim?

### What conditions?
What must be true for the value to materialize?

A credible value proposition does not promise outcomes without understanding dependencies.

---

# 8. Solution Storytelling Framework

Use the narrative:

**Today → Friction → Insight → Future → Solution → Experience → Value → Journey**

### Today
What does the current experience look like?

### Friction
Where does value leak?

### Insight
What needs to change?

### Future
What could the experience become?

### Solution
What enables that future?

### Experience
What would users/stakeholders actually experience?

### Value
What business outcome follows?

### Journey
How could the client move from current to future state?

---

# 9. Demonstration Framework

A strong demo follows the client's journey.

### Before
Establish the business problem.

### During
Demonstrate only capabilities relevant to the problem.

### After
Show the resulting experience and business outcome.

Use:

**Tell → Show → Explain → Relate → Validate**

Do not demonstrate every available feature.

> **Demo the future the client wants, not the product catalog.**

# 11. Objection Handling

Treat objections as information.

Use:

**Listen → Clarify → Acknowledge → Diagnose → Respond → Validate**

Typical objection categories:

- Value
- Cost
- Risk
- Feasibility
- Integration
- Security
- Data
- Adoption
- Timeline
- Capability
- Vendor dependency
- Change impact

Do not respond to an objection before understanding what is underneath it.

---

# 12. Value vs Price

Price should be understood in the context of value, risk and feasibility.

Explore:

- Business outcome
- Cost of current-state friction
- Potential efficiency
- Risk reduction
- Revenue / service impact where applicable
- Time-to-value
- Implementation effort
- Adoption requirements
- Ongoing operating cost
- Dependencies and assumptions

Never create artificial ROI claims.

Use evidence, assumptions and clearly stated uncertainty.

---

# 13. Solution Feasibility Model

Before making a commitment, validate:

### Business fit
Does it solve the actual problem?

### Functional fit
Can the capability support the required process?

### Technical fit
Can it work within the target architecture?

### Integration fit
Can required systems exchange information reliably?

### Data fit
Is required data available and usable?

### Security fit
Can identity, authorization and controls be satisfied?

### Delivery fit
Can it realistically be implemented?

### Operational fit
Can the organization sustain it?

### Commercial fit
Are licensing and commercial assumptions understood?

> **Never make a presales commitment that delivery cannot realistically support.**

# 14. Proposal / Response Architecture

A strong proposal should connect:

**Client Need → Understanding → Solution → Architecture → Delivery Approach → Value → Risks → Assumptions → Evidence → Next Step**

For RFP/RFI responses, maintain traceability between:

**Requirement → Response → Capability → Evidence → Assumption**

This makes the proposal defensible.

# 15. Presales Quality Model

Evaluate a presales engagement across:

### Relevance
Does the solution address the actual problem?

### Credibility
Are claims supported?

### Clarity
Can stakeholders understand the proposition?

### Feasibility
Can delivery realistically execute it?

### Value
Is the expected outcome articulated?

### Differentiation
Is the relevant distinction clear?

### Evidence
Are examples and assumptions defensible?

### Trust
Does the engagement increase confidence?

### Decision Readiness
Does the client have enough information for the next decision?

# 16. Presales Troubleshooting

### Client is disengaged
Revisit whether the conversation is addressing the client's problem.

### Demo receives little response
Validate whether the demonstrated capability is relevant.

### Price dominates the conversation
Reconnect the discussion to outcomes, assumptions and total value.

### Technical objections dominate
Bring appropriate solution architecture and technical evidence.

### Stakeholders disagree
Map their differing outcomes and decision criteria.

### Solution is attractive but infeasible
Surface constraints honestly and reshape the proposition.

### Delivery team challenges the proposal
Treat the feedback as a feasibility signal and correct commitments.

### Client asks for unsupported capability
Distinguish standard capability, configuration, customization, roadmap and unknowns clearly.

# 20. PSL Rule Book — Operationalized

### Do
- Start with the client's problem.
- Listen before pitching.
- Tell stories and demonstrate outcomes.
- Position value clearly.
- Validate feasibility with delivery and architecture stakeholders.
- Tailor the message to the audience.
- Understand sector-specific decision drivers.
- Build trust through evidence.
- Surface assumptions and risks.
- Use persuasion in service of truth.

### Don't
- Lead with features.
- Talk over the client.
- Recite product catalogs.
- Compete on price alone.
- Make unsupported commitments.
- Treat presales as a sales demo.
- Assume every industry buys the same way.
- Demo irrelevant features.
- Hide limitations or dependencies.
- Promise what delivery cannot realistically provide.
